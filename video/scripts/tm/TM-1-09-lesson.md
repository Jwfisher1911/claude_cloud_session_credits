# Daily Battle Drills

## What you will be able to do

- Complete one revenue action at first light, before you open email.
- Pick exactly three Mission-Essential Tasks each day and keep admin below the line.
- Build a weekly rhythm with set blocks for revenue, delivery, financial review, and rest.
- Track four numbers each working day: contacts made, offers made, invoices sent, and money collected.
- Run a 30-minute after-action review each Friday and carry one improvement into next week.

## Lesson 1: The first-light task

A battle rhythm is the repeating schedule a military unit runs each day and each week. Meetings, reports, and actions happen at set times, so nobody wastes effort deciding what comes next. TM 1-09 builds a daily battle rhythm for your business. It starts with the first action of each day.

First light is the moment the sky gets bright enough to see. Units plan their first action of the day around it. Your first-light task is one revenue action you complete before you open email, read texts, or touch admin.

A revenue action moves a customer closer to paying you. Examples:

- Call a past customer and offer a repeat service.
- Send a quote to a prospect.
- Follow up on an open proposal.
- Send an invoice for finished work.
- Ask a satisfied customer for a referral.

Email fills a morning with other people's priorities. Admin feels productive and brings in no money. Put one revenue action ahead of both. Sales work then happens every working day, no matter how the rest of the day goes.

1. Pick the task the night before. Write it on a card or on the first line of tomorrow's page.
2. Make it specific. Name the person, the action, and the deadline. Write "Call Maria Lopez about the spring cleanup quote," not "Do sales."
3. Keep it under 30 minutes. A small task gets done. A large one gets postponed.
4. Turn on do-not-disturb. Leave email, texts, and social media closed until the task is done.
5. Complete the task.
6. Mark it done and write the result in one line.

**Example:** Dana runs a two-person lawn care company. Her old morning started with coffee, the inbox, supplier emails, and the scheduling app. Sales calls slid to the afternoon, when her crew needed her in the field. She set one rule. At 6:30 a.m., before email, she calls one customer from last season's list and offers a spring aeration booking. She keeps the list on a clipboard next to the coffee maker. The call takes ten minutes. Some mornings nobody answers. She leaves a voicemail and marks the task done. The action counts. The outcome belongs to the customer.

> **Field note:** Keep a first-light list of 20 names: past customers, open quotes, and referral partners. When you pick tomorrow's task at night, pull the next name from the list. Add names as you meet people. You will not waste morning time deciding whom to call.

**Your task today:** Write tomorrow's first-light task on a card. Include the person's name, the action, and the time you will do it. Place the card on top of your phone or laptop before you go to bed.

## Lesson 2: Three Mission-Essential Tasks

In the military, Mission-Essential Tasks are the tasks a unit must perform to accomplish its mission. Training time and resources go to those tasks before anything else.

Your business gets exactly three Mission-Essential Tasks, or METs, per day. Not two. Not five. Three.

At least one of the three must touch revenue. A revenue-touching task moves money toward the business. Finding customers, making offers, delivering paid work, billing, and collecting all count.

Everything else is admin. Admin goes below the line. Draw a line under your three METs and list admin underneath. Admin waits until the METs are done or until a set admin block on your calendar.

Why three? Three tasks fit inside a working day with room for phone calls and surprises. A list of ten leaves you guessing which task mattered. If you finish all three, pull from below the line.

1. At the end of each working day, write tomorrow's first-light task at the top of a card.
2. Under it, write three METs. Number them 1, 2, and 3 in priority order.
3. Mark each revenue-touching task with an R. At least one task carries an R.
4. Draw a line under task 3.
5. Write admin items below the line.
6. Work the METs in order. Touch admin after task 3 or during your admin block.
7. At the end of the day, check off what you finished. Move any unfinished MET to tomorrow's card. It takes one of tomorrow's three slots.

**Example:** Marcus, a Marine veteran, runs a mobile notary service. Here is his Tuesday card:

- First light: Text the title company office manager to confirm Thursday's two closings.
- MET 1 (R): Visit two law offices downtown and leave rate sheets.
- MET 2 (R): Complete the 2:00 p.m. loan signing.
- MET 3: Renew his errors and omissions insurance before Friday.
- Below the line: Reply to email, update the mileage log, order stamp ink, file journal entries.

Two of his three METs touch revenue. The insurance renewal brings in no money, but his title company clients require the coverage, so it earns a slot. The mileage log waits below the line.

> **Field note:** A hard deadline turns admin into a Mission-Essential Task. When a license renewal, insurance renewal, or tax filing date arrives, promote it to a MET on the due date or the day before. Ask your CPA or the issuing agency for your exact deadlines and write them on your calendar.

**Your task today:** Fill out tomorrow's card. Write one first-light task, three METs with the revenue task marked R, a line under task 3, and your admin list below the line.

## Lesson 3: The weekly rhythm

Your daily card sets the day. Your weekly rhythm sets the week. It assigns each block of time to one of four jobs:

- **Revenue blocks:** Prospecting, quotes, sales calls, follow-up, and networking.
- **Delivery blocks:** The paid work your customers bought.
- **Financial review:** One block each week to send invoices, check collections, review the four daily numbers, and look at your bank account.
- **Rest:** At least one full day off.

Mixing jobs wastes time. Switching from a customer site to a sales call to an invoice and back to a site costs focus and drive time. Group like work together.

Rest goes on the grid before anything else. A day off you plan gets protected. A day off you hope for gets filled with work.

Your first-light task happens on every working day, delivery days included. A delivery day still starts with one revenue action.

1. Mark your rest day on the grid. Pick at least one full day.
2. Pick your revenue blocks. Choose times when your customers answer the phone. Plan at least two half-day blocks per week.
3. Pick your delivery blocks. Group paid work on these days.
4. Set one financial review block. Friday afternoon works well, and it pairs with the after-action review in Lesson 5.
5. Write the rhythm on a Monday to Sunday grid. Post it where you see it each morning.
6. Hold the rhythm for four weeks before you change it. Use your Friday reviews to decide what to change.

**Example:** Ray, an Air Force veteran, owns a one-truck HVAC contractor. Emergency calls broke every plan he made. He built this rhythm:

| Day | Morning | Afternoon |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Revenue: call back last week's estimates | Delivery: scheduled maintenance |
| Tuesday | Delivery | Delivery |
| Wednesday | Revenue: visit two property managers | Delivery |
| Thursday | Delivery | Delivery |
| Friday | Delivery | Financial review and after-action review |
| Saturday | On call for emergencies | On call for emergencies |
| Sunday | Rest | Rest |

Emergency calls go into delivery blocks. When one lands on a revenue morning, Ray moves the revenue block to the next open afternoon in the same week. He does not drop it.

> **Field note:** If your work has a busy season, write two rhythms. Use one for peak months and one for slow months. In slow months, move more blocks to revenue so you fill the calendar for the next peak.

**Your task today:** Draw a Monday to Sunday grid for next week with a morning and afternoon row for each day. Mark rest, revenue, delivery, and one financial review block, in this order.

## Lesson 4: The four daily numbers

Your MET card tells you what you did. The four daily numbers tell you whether the work moved money. Record these four each working day:

- **Contacts made:** Conversations or direct messages with a specific prospect, past customer, or referral partner about your business. A mass email does not count.
- **Offers made:** Specific proposals with a price, sent or spoken to one person.
- **Invoices sent:** Bills sent for delivered work or for deposits due.
- **Money collected:** Dollars received in your business account.

The numbers show where your sales process stalls:

- Many contacts and few offers: your conversations stop before you name a price.
- Many offers and few invoices: customers decline, or work sits unfinished.
- Many invoices and low collections: your billing follow-up has a gap.

Feelings mislead. A busy week feels like a good week. The numbers show what happened.

1. Write your definition of each number for your business. Decide what counts as a contact before you start counting.
2. Pick one tracker and keep it in one place: a paper card, a notebook, or a spreadsheet.
3. Record the four numbers at the end of each working day. This takes two minutes.
4. Write zero when the count is zero. Do not skip a day.
5. Total each number on Friday for your financial review and after-action review.

**Example:** Priya, a military spouse, runs a bookkeeping practice from home. In one week she recorded 14 contacts, 3 offers, 6 invoices sent, and payments on 2 of those invoices. Her contacts and offers held steady. Her collections lagged. Four invoices sat unpaid past her payment terms. She added a MET for Monday morning: call each client with an open invoice and confirm a payment date. Without the numbers, she would have spent Monday hunting for new clients and left the unpaid invoices alone.

> **Field note:** Track money collected, not money promised. A signed agreement counts as an offer won. Money in your business account counts as money collected. If you need help setting up your books or reading your cash flow, talk to a CPA or a bookkeeper.

**Your task today:** Write your definition of each of the four numbers on one card. Before you stop work, record today's four numbers on the card, zeros included.

## Lesson 5: The Friday after-action review

An after-action review, or AAR, is a structured discussion held after a mission or training event. The unit compares the plan to the result and decides what to keep and what to change. It focuses on facts, not blame.

Your Friday AAR answers five questions:

1. **Planned:** What did I plan to do this week?
2. **Happened:** What happened?
3. **Why:** Why was there a difference?
4. **Sustain:** What worked and should continue?
5. **Improve:** What one thing will I change next week?

Keep it to 30 minutes. Write the answers down. A review held in your head disappears by Monday.

Pick one improvement, not five. One change you make beats five changes you forget.

1. Block 30 minutes on Friday afternoon, after your financial review.
2. Gather the week's MET cards, your weekly grid, and your four-numbers tracker.
3. Read last week's AAR. Check whether you made last week's improvement.
4. Write what you planned, using the weekly grid and the cards.
5. Write what happened, using your completed cards and the week's totals for the four numbers.
6. For each gap, write the cause in one sentence. State facts.
7. List one to three items to sustain.
8. Write one improvement as a task with a day and time. Put it on next week's grid.
9. File the form in one folder or binder.

**Example:** Andre, a Navy veteran, runs a small airport shuttle and transportation service. Here is his Friday AAR:

| Question | Andre's answer |
|---|---|
| Planned | Two revenue blocks. Eight contacts with hotel front desks. |
| Happened | One revenue block held. Four contacts. One offer. Five rides delivered. |
| Why | A van repair on Tuesday took the Wednesday revenue block. I did not reschedule it. |
| Sustain | First-light task on all five working days. Four numbers recorded each day. |
| Improve | When a block moves, write the new time on the grid within the hour. |

Andre's improvement targets the cause, not the symptom. The van repair was outside his control. The missing reschedule was inside it.

> **Field note:** Run the review in a good week too. A strong week shows you what to sustain. If you have a business partner or spouse involved in the business, run the AAR together and let each person answer the five questions before you discuss them.

**Your task today:** Put a 30-minute block on this Friday's calendar labeled "AAR." Write the five questions on one page of your notebook so the form is ready when Friday arrives.

## Key terms

- **Battle rhythm:** A repeating daily and weekly schedule of set tasks, reviews, and meetings. In a business, it tells you what work happens on which day.
- **First light:** The time in the morning when the sky gets bright enough to see. Military units plan their first action of the day around it.
- **First-light task:** One revenue action you complete each working day before you open email or touch admin.
- **Mission-Essential Task (MET):** One of the three most important tasks you must finish today. At least one touches revenue.
- **Revenue-touching task:** Any task which moves money toward the business, such as finding customers, making offers, delivering paid work, billing, or collecting.
- **Below the line:** The admin list written under your three METs. These tasks wait until the METs are done.
- **Delivery:** Doing the paid work your customer bought.
- **Financial review:** A weekly block to send invoices, check collections, review your numbers, and check your bank account.
- **Four daily numbers:** Contacts made, offers made, invoices sent, and money collected, recorded each working day.
- **After-action review (AAR):** A short written review comparing what you planned with what happened, why they differ, and what to change.
- **Sustain:** An AAR item which worked and should continue.
- **Improve:** An AAR item you will change next week.
